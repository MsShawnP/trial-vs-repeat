## Penetration went up. The brand was dying.

Here are two product launches from the same specialty brand. Both looked like wins in their first quarter. One of them was a business. The other was a receipt for trade spend.

The first — call it the snack item — had a big launch: nearly a quarter of all panel households tried it. The second, a pantry staple, drew less than half that trial. On a penetration dashboard, the snack item is the star. It's the one that moves the quarterly reach number, the one that gets the follow-on display budget.

Now count who came back.

Of the households that tried the snack item, **about one in seven repeated** within a year. Of the households that tried the pantry staple, **more than half did**. The star was a promotion — expensive sampling that collapses the moment acquisition spend stops. The quiet one was a brand.

This is the trap in penetration data, and it is not subtle once you look for it: **penetration can rise every quarter while the business erodes.** New buyers pour in from a price feature, a display, a viral moment. Almost none repeat. Reach climbs. Then the spend stops, and the bucket — which was leaking the whole time — empties.

### Why the dashboard can't see it

A distribution or velocity report counts transactions. It cannot tell a first-ever buyer from a loyal one, because it has no household identity. Penetration needs panel data. And even with a panel, a naïve repeat rate lies about your most recent buyers: they haven't had time to come back yet. Count them as non-repeaters and you'll condemn a healthy launch for the crime of being new.

So Leaky Bucket does two things most repeat numbers don't. It cohorts buyers by the quarter they first bought and shows retention as a triangle — the missing corner is the time that hasn't elapsed yet, shown honestly rather than smoothed over. And every summary repeat number applies a **maturity cutoff**: only triers whose full repeat window has passed are counted. The window is a parameter, because a snack bite and a pantry staple repeat on different clocks.

### The one chart that matters

Plot each launch by how many households tried it and how many came back. Draw a line at the repeat rate that separates a brand from a promotion. The snack item sits far to the right — huge trial — and well below the line. The pantry staple sits modestly to the left and comfortably above it.

Same trial story. Opposite endings. That is the difference between a brand and a promotion, and it is invisible until you measure repeat.
